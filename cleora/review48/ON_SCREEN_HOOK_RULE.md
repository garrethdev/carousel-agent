# What the on-screen hook has to do

The reader is a woman of about forty-five to fifty-five, with money, on
her phone. She reads the card as a fragment, in roughly half a second,
with no other context.

**The card must name something she already recognises, and say what it
does to her body.** That is the whole rule. The history is the reason she
keeps watching; it is not the reason she stops scrolling.

Her two worked examples:

> THE TEENAGE **ACNE CREAM** THAT REBUILDS **COLLAGEN**
> THE **EIGHT DOLLAR** ANTI-AGING **PRODUCT** WITH A REAL TRIAL

Both name a thing she can picture (an acne cream, a cheap product) and
the payoff for her own face. The price and the phrase "a real trial" do
extra work: they answer "is this for me" and "is this real".

## The failure mode

Naming the subject of the *story* instead of the subject of *her body*
produces a history headline that reads as a fragment about strangers:

| fails | why | fixed |
|---|---|---|
| THE SCHOOLGIRLS THEY TESTED IODINE ON | reads as a crime report about children | THE CHEAP MINERAL YOUR THYROID RUNS ON |
| RICKETS, COAL SMOKE, AND THE MAN NOBODY HEARD | three nouns, no benefit, no subject she owns | THE VITAMIN YOU ONLY GET FROM SKIN AND SUN |
| THE NURSE'S TEA THAT LOST BY ONE VOTE | a vote in Ontario is not her problem | (unfixable - see below) |

## When the hook cannot be fixed

A headline cannot rescue a story that is not about her. Nine of the
forty-eight are medical history with no route back to her own face,
weight or energy: cancer (x3), yellow fever, malaria, anaesthesia,
myasthenia gravis, brain tumours, bacterial infection.

The only hooks that would land on those imply a cancer or malaria claim.
Do not write them. Flag the story instead.

## Test before writing

1. Cover the story. Does the card still say something to a stranger?
2. Does it name a thing she recognises - a cream, a vitamin, a pill, a
   price, a part of her body?
3. Does it answer "why do I care" without her watching a second of video?

Three yeses or rewrite it.
